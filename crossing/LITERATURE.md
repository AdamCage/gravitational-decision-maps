# Decision boundaries under prototype attraction

The [preceding pilot](../RESEARCH.md) found that applying a fixed source-score readout after integration produced a 3.75-point synthetic balanced-accuracy loss relative to the readout at initialization. Almost the entire aggregate loss came from the held-out ring wedges. Applying the same comparison on Digits and Wine changed balanced accuracy by −0.14 points. Those observations motivate an audit of the boundary motion, with the pilot kept separate from tests registered for this study.

## What the experiment can identify

Let $h_t(x)=\arg\max_i S_i(\Phi_t(x))$ for fixed scores $S_i$ and a fitted flow $\Phi_t$. Compare $h_0$ with $h_t$ at the same observations. A change in accuracy comes only from points whose winning score changed. For a binary target under the evaluation law, write $\eta(x)=\Pr(Y=1\mid X=x)$. A switch from label $0$ to $1$ changes conditional error by $1-2\eta(x)$. The reverse switch changes it by $2\eta(x)-1$. A score crossing can therefore be predictable while its effect on class accuracy remains unidentified by the observed cores. The evaluation separates following the flow faithfully from improving the labels.

In a smooth binary setting with regular margin $m=\log(S_1/S_0)$, the class-1 region satisfies $A_t=\Phi_{-t}(\{m>0\})$. Assume the boundary and class densities satisfy the usual transport integrability conditions. For fixed joint class densities $p_0,p_1$ under the evaluation law, the Reynolds formula gives

$$
R'(0)=\int_{\partial A_0}(p_0-p_1)\,
\frac{D_Fm}{\|\nabla m\|}\,\mathrm dS.
$$

Indeed, the outward normal to $A_0$ is $-\nabla m/\|\nabla m\|$ and its velocity is $-F$. Balanced error uses $p_i=f_{X\mid Y=i}/2$ in the binary case. If the initial boundary is a regular Bayes boundary then $p_0=p_1$ there and the first variation vanishes, although transport may increase risk at positive time. The population derivative requires a density contrast at the boundary and cannot be read off the field on unlabeled test inputs. [Reddiger and Poirier, transport theorem and Corollary 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.03330)

For the base field, let $g_i=\nabla\log S_i$ and $\alpha_i=S_i/\sum_k S_k$. At an $i,j$ tie with $\alpha_i=\alpha_j=q$, the normal signed score derivative is

$$
D_F\log(S_i/S_j)
=b^2\left[
q(\|g_i\|^2-\|g_j\|^2)
+ \sum_{k\ne i,j}\alpha_k(g_i-g_j)\cdot g_k
\right].
$$

In the binary case $q=1/2$. With shared isotropic precision this compares the squared distances from the boundary point to the two local class prototype means, up to a positive scale. The multiclass contribution need not be small just because the third class has a lower score. At a one-dimensional tie take $\alpha=(0.34,0.34,0.32)$ and $g=(1,0.4,-2)$. The third class reverses the normal sign relative to using the two leading classes alone. Unit-bandwidth Gaussian scores at $x=0$ realize these values with centers $g_i$ and masses proportional to $\alpha_i\exp(g_i^2/2)$. A patched field must use its actual $F$ in the derivative.

An additive function shared by all class log scores leaves their margins and the static predictions unchanged. It adds a gradient term to the marginal field. A matched comparison therefore fixes the full prototype potential, including terms that cancel in score differences. This is the same distinction between posterior and marginal energy made in the joint energy-based model. [Grathwohl et al., Eq. 7 and discussion](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1912.03263#page=3)

## Falsification and applicability

1. **Score crossing versus classifier crossing.** A regular binary tie and a strict sign change imply crossing. A multiclass endpoint label requires comparisons against every competitor. An observed endpoint change answers a different question from first entry or any crossing during the interval.
2. **Tangency and recrossing.** For margin $m(x,y)=y$ and field $F(x,y)=(1,x)$, the Lie derivative at $(0,0)$ is zero and the trajectory immediately has $m=t^2/2$. A degenerate margin such as $m(x)=x^3$ under $F=1$ crosses with a vanishing first derivative. For the attractive field $F(x)=1-x$, the path from $0$ passes the two roots of $m(x)=(x-1/4)(x-3/4)$ before time $2$. Both endpoint signs are positive. These examples test tangency, regularity and the difference between an event and an endpoint.
3. **Short-time validity.** Along a trajectory, a Taylor remainder of size at most $Bh^2/2$ is valid when $B$ bounds $\left|F^\top D^2m F+\nabla m^\top(DF)F\right|$ on the complete path tube. Strict separated endpoint intervals support a sign claim. Loose bounds, singular ties and unresolved numerical references should remain visible. Include the same-score one-Euler-step rule as a cheap comparator.
4. **Selective coverage.** Report the denominator of every certificate. Include coverage on all eligible inputs, the fraction of reference flips captured, error among accepted references, and how frequently references stay ambiguous. Tabulate crossing directions separately. A rare accepted subset or the accuracy of an all-no-flip predictor can obscure weak applicability.
5. **Shell controls.** A known-centered shell is a structural positive control. Fitted-centroid or fitted-circle scores require core-only estimation. Apply matched static and dynamic methods to the same features and score function. Nonconcentric and radial-label-rule violations test the scope of the prior. Global shape claims require continuous-domain arguments or an explicit discretization qualification.

The publishable question is whether an analytic crossing test explains when a class-prototype flow preserves or changes a matched decision, with useful coverage at a declared horizon and predictable failure modes across independent benchmarks. The density flow, the first Lie derivative, the Taylor inequality and topological invariance are established tools. The evidence for a contribution would come from nontrivial field-specific bounds or decomposition, success and failure predictions specified before evaluation, and a class-risk analysis that exposes when crossing harms and when it helps.
